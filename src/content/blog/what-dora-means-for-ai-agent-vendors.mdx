If you sell an AI agent to banks in the EU, you have probably already met DORA, even if nobody used the name. It shows up as a longer questionnaire, a new contract schedule, or a risk team that wants to know exactly what your agent will be allowed to do.

This post explains where that comes from, and what you can do about it.

## DORA in one paragraph

The Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, has applied since 17 January 2025. It sets common rules for how financial firms manage technology risk: their own systems, their incidents, their testing, and the technology suppliers they depend on. That last part, ICT third-party risk, is where you come in.

## Why it lands on you

DORA's obligations sit with the bank. But a bank cannot hand off responsibility for risk that comes from a supplier. It has to understand, monitor and be able to evidence how its suppliers behave, and it does that through you.

In practice that means three things.

1. **You go in the register.** Banks must keep a register of information on their contracts with ICT third-party providers. Expect detailed questions about what your service does, which of the bank's functions it supports, and where data goes.
2. **Your contract gets longer.** DORA lists terms these contracts must contain. Where your agent supports a critical or important function, they include rights of access, inspection and audit, and a plan for exit.
3. **Oversight continues after signature.** The bank has to keep showing its supervisor that the risk is under control, so the questions don't stop at go-live.

## Why agents get extra scrutiny

A traditional SaaS tool shows a human some information and the human acts. An agent acts. It issues a refund, reads a file, changes a limit.

Risk teams are generally willing to let agents do more, but only within defined tolerances, and only with proof those tolerances held. That is a different question from "do you have controls?" It is "show me what happened."

## What usually gets offered, and what's missing

Most vendors answer with some mix of:

- **Certifications** such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001. Useful for getting shortlisted. They describe controls over a past period, not what happened for this bank last week.
- **Questionnaire answers.** They record what you say your system does.
- **Logs.** They show calls happened. They rarely show who authorised each action, or whether anything is missing.

None of these is wrong. They just answer a different question from the one a risk team is now asking.

## What helps

Evidence that answers "what did the agent do, and who allowed it?" has a few properties:

- **Per action.** Each thing the agent did is recorded individually.
- **Attributed.** Each record names the person or the policy version that allowed it.
- **Tamper-evident.** Records are signed and chained, so edits and deletions show.
- **Checked for gaps.** A coverage check compares what the agent attempted with what was recorded, so anything missing shows.
- **Per customer.** Each bank sees evidence about its own customers, organised by the DORA areas it reports against.

That is what we are building at Shoirly. Whether or not you use us, those properties are a useful test for any evidence you plan to put in front of a bank.

## A practical checklist before your next bank deal

- Know whether the bank treats your service as supporting a critical or important function. It changes the contract terms.
- Prepare a short description of exactly what actions your agent can take, and what authorises each one.
- Decide how you would show a bank what your agent did for its customers over a given month.
- Read the audit and access clauses in your draft contract with that answer in mind.
