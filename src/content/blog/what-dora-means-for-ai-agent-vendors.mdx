If you are selling an AI agent to a bank in the EU, DORA is likely to become part of the sales process. You may see it in a due-diligence questionnaire, a request to explain where data is processed, or a contract that asks for audit and exit rights.

The distinction matters: DORA puts obligations on the bank, not automatically on every company that sells it software. The bank must manage supplier risk, so questions reach you through assessments, monitoring and contract terms. That does not make your startup a regulated financial entity.

## DORA, briefly

The Digital Operational Resilience Act is Regulation (EU) 2022/2554. It has applied since 17 January 2025. It sets requirements for financial entities' management of information and communication technology (ICT) risk, including supplier relationships.

For the supplier relationship, start with Articles 28 and 30 of the [Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj), covering ICT third-party risk and contractual arrangements. They are useful reading, but they do not turn a vendor into the bank's compliance officer. The financial entity remains responsible for its own compliance and for managing its ICT third-party risk.

## What the bank has to do

The bank must manage supplier risk as part of its wider ICT risk management. It has to assess proposed arrangements before entering them and keep an up-to-date register of its ICT provider contracts. The register records the relationships and services it depends on. It is not a public vendor list or a certificate you can obtain to declare yourself DORA-compliant.

Before entering an arrangement, the bank considers whether the service supports a critical or important function, the provider's suitability, and risks including concentration and conflicts of interest. Expect questions about what your service does, how the bank will use it, what data it touches, where processing takes place and whether other providers are involved.

Article 30 sets out terms for contracts covering ICT services. These include a description of services and processing locations, provisions for availability and data security, incident assistance, cooperation with competent authorities and termination rights. Wording and applicability depend on the arrangement, so review the actual contract with qualified counsel.

Where the service supports a critical or important function, Article 30 adds requirements. These include service levels, reporting and notification arrangements, business-continuity measures, and effective access, inspection and audit rights for the financial entity, an appointed third party or the competent authority. The contract must also address exit and transition, including a suitable transition period. This is why a risk team may ask about audit evidence and how the bank could move away from a service, not just whether the service is secure today.

## What flows through to the vendor

To carry out its duties, the bank needs information and contractual rights. A vendor may be asked about service boundaries, data flows, security controls, incidents, subcontractors, continuity, audit and exit. These expectations appear in contracts, procurement and service reviews.

These are indirect expectations arising from the bank's obligations and contract, not a blanket statement that the vendor has the same duties as the bank. The service, function and contract matter. Be precise about what exists today, what is planned, and what depends on the bank's systems. Do not promise capabilities you cannot meet.

## “Critical or important” is not the same as a CTPP

There are two labels that are easy to confuse. A bank assesses whether a function is critical or important for that bank. That assessment influences the bank's risk management and the additional contract requirements for services supporting that function. It is about the function and the particular arrangement, not a general status awarded to a vendor.

Separately, Article 31 provides for European Supervisory Authorities to designate certain ICT providers as critical third-party providers (CTPPs), based on criteria that include their systemic importance to financial entities and the ability to substitute their services. A vendor appearing in a bank's register, or supporting a function the bank considers critical or important, is not for that reason alone a formally designated CTPP. The Article 31 oversight framework applies to providers that receive that formal designation. Most early-stage vendors should focus first on being clear and credible in each customer's assessment and contract.

## Why agent vendors should prepare evidence

An agent can take actions through connected tools, not only return information to a person. For a bank, the practical questions are specific: What could the agent do? What authorised that action? What happened when a request fell outside its permissions? If there is an incident or an audit, what can the vendor and the bank reconstruct?

DORA's third-party provisions do not prescribe one standard event record for AI agents, or require a particular signing method. Still, a useful evidence trail can make a vendor's answers easier to examine. It might connect each action to its timestamp, the human approval or policy that authorised it, the relevant service or customer, and the result. It should make changes detectable and make clear what the record can and cannot establish. Access should be limited to the people who need it, and the approach should respect the bank's data-handling and retention requirements.

Completeness deserves care. Ordinary application logs may show recorded calls, but not always whether any attempted actions went unrecorded. If your system has a way to compare attempted activity with captured records, explain what it checks and what the result means. A coverage check can show whether anything went unrecorded; it should not be described as proof that no event was missed.

Operational evidence is not a substitute for security controls, independent assurance, contract review or the bank's own work. Certifications, questionnaires and logs can all help, but answer different questions: an assessment describes controls, a questionnaire describes the service, and an event record helps explain an action.

## A practical checklist for your next bank conversation

Before you agree to a pilot or production contract, be ready to answer these questions clearly:

- What functions will the agent perform for this bank, and which actions can it take without a person in the loop?
- What authorises each action? Can you distinguish a human approval from a policy decision, and identify which policy applied?
- What information is processed, where does processing take place, and which subcontractors are involved? Confirm the answer with the people who operate your service.
- What can you provide about incidents, service continuity, audit access and exit? Check that your operational capability matches the words in the proposed contract.
- If asked about an event, what evidence can you provide, for what period, and what are its limitations? Do not imply it is complete or independently verifiable unless you can support that claim.
- Has the bank decided whether its function is critical or important? Ask how that assessment affects the service requirements and contract, rather than assuming a category for yourself.

Keep answers consistent across questionnaires, security materials and contracts. If you do not yet have an answer, say so and agree a next step. That is more useful than a broad assurance that your system “logs everything”.
